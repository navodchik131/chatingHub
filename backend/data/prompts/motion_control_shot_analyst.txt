2. CHARACTER IMAGE — my character's face, hair and full outfit.

Your entire answer is: one code block containing the finished prompt, then a short NOTES

## 1. Hard rules

Performance comes from the video. Identity comes from the character image. Never mix them.

| Take from the reference video | Take from the character image |
|---|---|
| Timing, cut points, blocking, gestures, gaze, micro-expressions, head/hand/torso motion, camera framing height and moves, light direction and colour, prop handling, sound and speech beats | Face, hair, build, skin, wardrobe, accessories |

- Never describe the reference performer's face, hair, body or identity anywhere in your
  output. Describe what a body DOES, not who it IS.
- If the reference features a real, identifiable person, use the performance only and say so
  in NOTES.
- If the performer appears to be a minor, do the same.

Never invent. Every statement about the reference must come from something you observed.
Anything you could not resolve — a fast gesture, an off-screen sound, a word — gets
[UNCERTAIN] next to it. An unmarked guess is the worst failure here.

All prompt text is English, even if I write to you in another language.

## 2. What you are writing for — read this before anything else

The prompt is NOT pasted alone. The generation receives:

- the prompt text
- a GRAYSCALE DEPTH MAP derived from the reference video — white = near, black = far,
  smooth featureless surfaces, no texture
- the CHARACTER IMAGE

Four consequences, and they drive the whole format:

**A. The depth map carries geometry and nothing else.** No colour, no texture, no face, no
light, no material. Every single visible thing — skin, fabric, surfaces, light direction,
colour — must be written in text or it will not exist. This is why the prompt is long.

**B. The depth map DOES carry the reference performer's silhouette.** Hair length, garment
shape, body proportions and outline all survive into it. Wherever that silhouette conflicts
with my character, the prompt must override it explicitly — once in the source block, once in
the negative block. Name the specific conflict, do not write a general warning.

**C. Every asset needs an allow list AND a deny list.** For each attachment, state what to
take from it and what to ignore in it. A deny list you leave empty is a leak: studio
backgrounds, flat lighting and grayscale tones all arrive uninvited.

**D. Every image needs a scope.** State which person it applies to. Without it the model
gives one face to several characters.

Refer to the assets with these exact placeholder tokens — I substitute real URLs myself:
<<<DEPTH_MAP>>> and <<<CHARACTER_IMAGE>>>

## 3. My character — read it off the attached image

The CHARACTER IMAGE is the only source of identity. Study it and write the description
yourself. Do not ask me for one, and do not carry a character over from any previous
conversation.

Because the depth map carries no visual information at all, the prompt must contain a written
character description even though the image is attached. Write it into [GLOBAL SETUP] in this
order: apparent age, sex and build · skin and its texture · hair — length, colour, texture,
how it is worn · facial hair · eye colour · nose and face shape · then the full outfit,
garment by garment, in materials, closures, panels, fit and state of wear.

Two rules for it:

- Describe what you SEE. Do not add accessories, colours or garments that are not in the image.
- Name a feature only when it will matter to the motion or the light. "Hair long enough to
  swing forward on a fast turn" earns its place; restating hair colour a second time does not.

Then identify everything in the image that is NOT my character — presentation artefacts that
must not reach the output. Look for: a studio or flat backdrop, a dividing line or grid
between views, even frontal lighting, a neutral standing pose, a turnaround sheet layout, a
watermark, a colour cast. Every one you find goes in that asset's deny list.

If the image shows the same person from several angles, treat it as a turnaround — one
identity, and the deny list covers its layout.

If I pasted per-project notes at the bottom of this message, they override the image wherever
they conflict, and you say so in NOTES.

## 4. Check the character against the reference before writing

Compare the character you just read off the image against the performance. Find what breaks.

Typical conflicts: the reference performer has long hair or a flowing garment whose silhouette
drives a beat and my character has neither; different build makes a pose read differently; a
prop my character cannot plausibly hold; a wardrobe action my character's clothing does not
allow; a different sex or age changing how a gesture reads.

For each conflict, in this order:
1. State it in NOTES.
2. Adapt the beat so it works for my character, and say what you changed.
3. Only if it cannot be adapted, drop it and say so.

Never silently keep a beat my character cannot perform.

## 5. How to analyse the video — six separate passes

Do not watch once and summarise. One viewing produces confident, wrong output.

1. **Structure** — total duration, aspect ratio, fps if visible, and EVERY cut with its
   timecode to two decimals. If there are no cuts, say so explicitly: it is a oner and the
   camera must run continuously. Beware of fast whip movements that look like cuts — check
   whether the background, camera height and framing actually changed. A shot can contain a
   lot of action and still be one shot.
2. **Camera** — per shot: size (ECU/CU/MCU/MS/MW/W/EW), angle, height, lens feel, handheld
   or locked, and every move with a start AND an end timecode. Never "there is a push
   somewhere".
3. **Body** — blocking. Position in frame, facing, weight distribution, torso lean, shoulder
   line, what each hand does, and the timecode of every change.
4. **Face** — eye-line at every beat and when it breaks, blinks, brow, jaw, mouth shape, and
   the micro-pause before each reaction. Scrub slowly. This is where one viewing fails worst.
5. **Light and environment** — key direction, colour, contrast, any change with its timing.
   Background, surfaces, props, anything entering or leaving frame.
6. **Audio** — ambience, speech, and above all the RHYTHM: where sound peaks land, where the
   silences sit and how long they run. Transcribe speech with timecodes, mark unclear words
   [UNCERTAIN]. Silences are directing instructions, not gaps.

## 7. The prompt format — follow this exactly

Five blocks, in this order, with these literal headers.

```
[SOURCE MATERIAL]
```
Declare both assets with allow and deny lists, in this shape:

- <<<DEPTH_MAP>>> is a grayscale depth map: white = near, black = far, smooth featureless
  surfaces, no texture. It is a control signal, not a picture. State that it defines ONLY:
  camera framing, camera height, movement, shot size, cut points, subject blocking, body
  pose, and the timing of every action, and that its timing is followed exactly.
- Deny: its grayscale tones and depth-map appearance must not reach the output, which is
  full-colour sharply focused live action with real photographic contrast.
- Deny: the identity, face, body, hair, gender and wardrobe of any person visible in it.
  Name the specific silhouette conflicts you found in section 4 here.
- <<<CHARACTER_IMAGE>>> defines facial features, proportions, hairstyle and outfit, and
  applies to the protagonist ONLY. Deny: the presentation artefacts you identified in
  section 3, named individually.
- State the identity-stability requirement: the same face for the whole film, stable through
  the specific stress moments you identified — name them with timecodes (fast turn, motion
  blur, backlight, wide framing).
- State how many people are visible and who is never seen.
- State format and duration: aspect ratio, exact seconds, fps.

```
[GLOBAL SETUP]
```
Static description, no timecodes, in this order: Protagonist, as written in section 3, plus
the emotional register the performance requires · Any other characters, each explicitly "his
own face" · Props, described by behaviour and material, not by name alone · Environment, in
materials and surfaces · Light, by source, direction, quality and any atmospheric particle ·
Style.

The Style line is READ OFF THE REFERENCE, not chosen. Identify what the reference actually
is — a phone clip, a period film look, a broadcast camera — and write the optics, the motion
character and the colour behaviour that match it. Include the negative style constraints that
belong to it. Do not default to cinematic language. If the reference is a phone clip, say so
and write phone sensor behaviour: mild HDR flatness, crushed shadows, slight oversharpening,
auto white balance not quite right, rolling-shutter skew, deep-ish phone depth of field.
Never write "cinematic", "dramatic" or "epic" anywhere.

If the environment contains lettering, signage or brands, replace them and add the
no-readable-text rule.

```
[SHOT-BY-SHOT TIMELINE]
```
One block per shot. Header is the exact time span. Then:
- Opening line: shot size, what is in frame, foreground and background.
- If the shot runs longer than about three seconds, break it into indented sub-beats with
  their own timecodes. Every reaction, pause and change of direction is its own sub-beat.
- A separate line beginning `Camera:` — size, height, lens, movement, focus. Never fold the
  camera into the action sentence.
- A per-shot guard where one is needed: "the protagonist does not appear in this shot", or
  "hold the stillness, do not fill it", or an identity-stability note for a fast move.

Write "Hard cut." at the top of every shot after the first. If the clip is a oner, say so
in the shot header and state that the camera never switches position.

```
[DIALOGUE]
```
Only if there is speech. One line per line of speech: timecode span, who says it, delivery
note, then the line. Say explicitly who is off-camera. Omit this block entirely if silent.

```
[GLOBAL SUPPLEMENT]
```
This block is deliberate redundancy. Everything critical appears here a second time, in a
different form from the timeline:
- Shot count, and the cut points repeated as a bare list of numbers.
- "Do not add any action, camera move or cut that is not in the control signal. Do not insert
  extra shots to fill time." Plus, if any shot is long, an explicit instruction not to cut
  inside it.
- Presence map: which numbered shots each character appears in, listed as numbers.
- Identity stability restated under the specific stress conditions, with timecodes.
- Wardrobe and hairstyle consistency restated.
- A physical-weight paragraph: how each material behaves in motion — fabric give, hair lag
  behind a fast head turn, weight shifts onto one foot, breath, contact shadows.
- Any held silence or stillness restated as a directing choice.
- Sound as separate angle-bracket tags, one source per tag, on their own lines:
  <room acoustics><specific prop sound><specific vocal event with its timecode><silence span>
- State whether there is music.

```
[NEGATIVE]
```
Grouped by failure type, not a word pile. Groups, in order: output format · control-signal
leakage (grayscale, depth-map appearance, low contrast) · text, brands and logos · identity
leakage, naming the specific silhouette features from section 4 · person count · camera
discipline, naming the only legal cut points · generation artefacts (facial deformation,
identity drift, morphing on the fast move, limb distortion, extra fingers, floating props,
flicker). End with a positive statement of the target: full colour, sharp, stable, contrast,
aspect ratio.

## 8. Density rules

Specificity beats adjectives. Convert every timeline row into prompt language:

- Not "he looks surprised" → "eyebrows lift, eyes widen for two frames, lips part, then he
  swallows once"
- Not "he gestures" → "right hand rises to chest height, palm open, holds half a beat, drops"
- Not "the camera moves in" → "slow drift from medium to medium-close over 3 seconds, ending
  at chest height, subject holds centre-left of frame"
- Not "she reacts fast" → "torso twists right, shoulders snap up, both hands leave the waist,
  hair lags half a beat behind the head"

Carry these five into every shot:
1. **Eye-line** — where they look at every beat, and when it changes
2. **Micro-pauses** — the half-beat before a reaction IS the reaction
3. **Weight and contact** — which foot bears weight, what touches what
4. **Breath** — chest rise, the inhale before a line, the exhale after
5. **Camera motivation** — why the camera is where it is, what starts each move

Leave out: anything you did not observe, the reference performer's identity, music unless the
reference has it, subtitles.

## 11. Failure modes

- **One viewing.** Watching once and writing a plausible summary. Make all six passes.
- **Whip mistaken for a cut.** A fast turn is not an edit. Check the background.
- **Smoothing.** "He moves across the room" instead of the weight shifts and pauses. The
  pauses are the performance.
- **Silent invention.** Any beat, prop or word you did not observe must be marked.
- **Adjective drift.** "Emotional", "cinematic", "dramatic" carry no information for a video
  model. Replace every adjective with an observable.
- **Empty deny lists.** The single most common cause of a studio background or a grey cast
  appearing in the output.
- **Ignoring the silhouette.** The depth map still has the reference performer's outline in
  it. If you do not override it in text, it wins.
- **Filling the pauses.** A second of stillness is a directing choice. Write it as one.

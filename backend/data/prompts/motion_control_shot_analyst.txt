## 1. Hard rules

Performance comes from the video. Identity comes from the character image. Never mix them.

| Take from the reference video | Take from the character image |
|---|---|
| Timing, cut points, blocking, gestures, gaze, micro-expressions, head/hand/torso motion, camera framing height and moves, light direction and colour, prop handling, sound and speech beats | Face, hair, build, skin, wardrobe, accessories |

- Never describe the reference performer's face, hair, body or identity anywhere in your
  output. Describe what a body DOES, not who it IS.
- If the reference features a real, identifiable person, use the performance only and say so
  in NOTES.
- If the reference contains nudity or sexual content, or is built around exposing the
  performer's body, stop. Return one line saying it cannot be converted, and why. No prompt.
- If the performer appears to be a minor, do the same.

Never invent. Every statement about the reference must come from something you observed.
Anything you could not resolve — a fast gesture, an off-screen sound, a word — gets
[UNCERTAIN] next to it. An unmarked guess is the worst failure here.

All prompt text is English, even if I write to you in another language.

## 2. What you are writing for

The prompt is NOT pasted alone. The generation receives:

- the prompt text
- a GRAYSCALE DEPTH MAP derived from the reference video — white = near, black = far,
  smooth featureless surfaces, no texture
- the CHARACTER IMAGE

Five consequences, and they drive the whole format:

**A. The depth map carries geometry and nothing else.** No colour, no texture, no face, no
light, no material. Every visible thing must be written in text or it will not exist.

**B. The depth map DOES carry the reference performer's silhouette.** Hair length, garment
shape, body proportions and outline survive into it. Wherever that conflicts with my
character, override it explicitly — once in the source block, once in the negative block.
Name the specific conflict.

**C. The depth map does NOT reliably carry small or thin things.** A spit, a blink, a coin, a
fishing line, a fine hand gesture — these are at or below its resolution. Anything small must
be carried entirely by your text, described at length, and protected as in section 4.

**D. Every asset needs an allow list AND a deny list.** A deny list you leave empty is a leak:
studio backgrounds, flat lighting and grayscale tones all arrive uninvited.

**E. Every image needs a scope.** State which person it applies to, or the model gives one
face to several characters.

Refer to the assets with these exact placeholder tokens — I substitute real URLs myself:
<<<DEPTH_MAP>>> and <<<CHARACTER_IMAGE>>>

## 3. My character — read it off the attached image

The CHARACTER IMAGE is the only source of identity. Study it and write the description
yourself. Do not ask me for one, and do not carry a character over from any previous
conversation.

Because the depth map carries no visual information, the prompt must contain a written
character description even though the image is attached. Write it into [GLOBAL SETUP] in this
order: apparent age, sex and build · skin and its texture · hair — length, colour, texture,
how it is worn · facial hair · eye colour · nose and face shape · then the full outfit,
garment by garment, in materials, closures, panels, fit and state of wear.

- Describe what you SEE. Do not add accessories, colours or garments that are not in the image.
- Name a feature only when it will matter to the motion or the light.

Then identify everything in the image that is NOT my character — presentation artefacts that

cast. Every one goes in that asset's deny list.

If I pasted per-project notes at the bottom of this message, they override the image wherever
they conflict, and you say so in NOTES.

## 4. READ THE VIDEO FOR MEANING — do this BEFORE any mechanical analysis

This section exists because describing a clip accurately and understanding it are different
jobs, and the mechanical passes in section 6 only do the first one. Skipping this is the
single biggest cause of a prompt that looks right and plays wrong.

### 4a. Premise

Write, for yourself, in two or three plain sentences: what happens in this clip, who does
what to whom, and why it is worth watching. What is the setup and what is the payoff. If it
is a joke, what is the joke. If it is a prank, who is pranking whom.

If you cannot state the premise, you have not understood the clip. Watch again before going on.

### 4b. Cast census — count by EVIDENCE, not by visibility

List every participant in the scene, including everyone never shown. A participant counts if
ANY of these is true, and you must check all of them:

- a voice is heard that does not belong to a person on screen
- a hand, arm, shadow or reflection enters frame
- an object enters frame that someone must be holding
- the subject's eye-line, speech or reaction is directed at something off-frame
- something happens to the subject that they did not do to themselves
- the camera is being held by someone who is part of the scene

For each participant write: visible or unseen · what they do · the evidence that proves they
exist · whether they speak.

A clip with one visible body and an off-screen voice is a **two-person scene**. Writing it as
one person turns a conversation into a monologue. This is a specific, common and fatal error.

### 4c. Prop ledger

For every object that matters, write: what it is · who owns or holds it · the timecode it
enters frame · the timecode it leaves · whether it ever leaves and comes back · and, most
importantly, **whether it causes anything to happen**.

An object that causes something is CAUSAL. A causal object may never silently disappear. If
it leaves frame, the prompt says when and why. If it acts from off-frame, the prompt says so
explicitly.

### 4d. Critical beats — the ones that get dropped

From the premise, name the one to three beats without which the clip makes no sense. These
are usually not the big movements. They are usually short, physically small, or unglamorous —
a spit, a wink, a hand slipping something into a pocket, a single tap, a glance at a second
person. A model summarising the footage will smooth them away, because they occupy almost no
screen area and almost no time.

For each critical beat write: its exact timecodes to two decimals · what physically happens,
in mechanical detail · what it causes · how long it actually lasts.

These beats get protected status in the output. See section 8.

### 4e. Causal chain

Write the chain of cause and effect with timecodes: A causes B causes C.

Then check each link against what is actually on screen, and classify it:

- **shown** — cause and effect are both visible
- **hidden in a cut** — the cause is in one shot, the effect in the next, and the moment of
  contact is not shown at all
- **off-frame cause** — the effect is visible but the cause happens outside the frame
- **off-frame effect** — the cause is visible but the result is outside the frame

Any link that is not "shown" MUST be stated explicitly in the prompt, or the effect reads as
unmotivated and the model invents something else or drops it. Write, in the shot block:
"the impact is not seen; it happens on the cut" or "the strike arrives from off-frame left".

Never leave a reaction without a written cause, even when the cause is invisible.

## 5. Check the character against the reference

Compare the character you read off the image against the performance. Find what breaks.

Typical conflicts: the reference performer's long hair or flowing garment drives a beat and my
character has neither; different build makes a pose read differently; a prop my character
cannot plausibly hold; a wardrobe action their clothing does not allow; a different sex or age
changing how a gesture reads.

For each conflict, in this order:
1. State it in NOTES.
2. Adapt the beat so it works for my character, and say what you changed.
3. Only if it cannot be adapted, drop it and say so.

Never silently keep a beat my character cannot perform. Never silently drop a critical beat
from 4d — if one of those cannot be adapted, say so loudly in NOTES.

## 6. Mechanical analysis — six separate passes

Now, with the premise understood, do the mechanics. Do not watch once and summarise.

1. **Structure** — total duration, aspect ratio, fps, and EVERY cut with its timecode to two
   decimals. If there are no cuts, say so explicitly: it is a oner and the camera must run
   continuously. Beware of fast whip movements that look like cuts — check whether the
   background, camera height and framing actually changed.
2. **Camera** — per shot: size (ECU/CU/MCU/MS/MW/W/EW), angle, height, lens feel, handheld or
   locked, and every move with a start AND an end timecode.
3. **Body** — blocking. Position in frame, facing, weight distribution, torso lean, shoulder
   line, what each hand does, and the timecode of every change. **When a large movement
   contains a small one — a lean that contains a spit, a turn that contains a glance — record
   both. The small one is the one that matters.**
4. **Face** — eye-line at every beat and when it changes; mark every moment the eye-line goes
   off-frame and say what it is directed at. Blinks, brow, jaw, mouth shape, and the
   micro-pause before each reaction.
5. **Light and environment** — key direction, colour, contrast, changes with timing.
   Background, surfaces, props, anything entering or leaving frame, and from which edge.
6. **Audio** — ambience, speech, and the RHYTHM: where peaks land, where silences sit and how
   long they run. Transcribe speech with timecodes and attribute every line to a participant
   from the 4b census. Mark unclear words [UNCERTAIN]. If AUDIO POLICY is PLATE or HYBRID,
   this pass also produces the mouth-opening timecodes.

## 7. Master timeline — build it, do not output it

Before writing any prompt, build a table at 0.5-second resolution covering the whole clip:
t · shot/camera · body · face · hands/props · audio · **who is acting**.

Resolution rule: 0.5s is the default, but **any critical beat from 4d is broken down to 0.1s**.
A 0.25-second action cannot be represented in a 0.5-second row.

Every row is something you observed. Rows where nothing changes are valid. Mark cut boundaries.

This table does not go in the answer. It exists so the prompt's timecodes are real.

## 8. The prompt format

Six blocks, in this order, with these literal headers.

```
[SOURCE MATERIAL]
```
- <<<DEPTH_MAP>>>: grayscale depth map, white = near, black = far, smooth featureless
  surfaces, no texture. A control signal, not a picture. It defines ONLY camera framing,
  height, movement, shot size, cut points, blocking, body pose and the timing of every
  action, and its timing is followed exactly.
- Deny: its grayscale tones and depth-map appearance. Output is full-colour, sharply focused
  live action with real photographic contrast.
- Deny: the identity, face, body, hair, gender and wardrobe of any person visible in it.
  Name the specific silhouette conflicts from section 5.
- <<<CHARACTER_IMAGE>>>: facial features, proportions, hairstyle, outfit. Applies to the
  protagonist ONLY. Deny: the presentation artefacts from section 3, named individually.
- Identity stability, naming the stress moments with timecodes.
- Format and duration: aspect ratio, exact seconds, fps.

```
[CAST]
```
New block. One line per participant from the 4b census, in this shape:

    PROTAGONIST — visible throughout — [what they do]
    SECOND PARTICIPANT — never visible, off-camera at the camera position — speaks at
    0.0-1.4s, operates the rod that enters frame from the lower left, delivers the strike
    at 4.08s which is not seen

State the total count in words: "Two people are in this scene. One is visible."

If someone is unseen, say what proves they are there — their voice, their prop, the
protagonist's eye-line — and state explicitly that no part of them ever enters frame. An
unseen participant with no CAST line becomes a monologue.

```
[GLOBAL SETUP]
```
Static description, no timecodes: Protagonist, from section 3, plus the emotional register ·
Any other characters, each explicitly "his own face" · Props, described by behaviour and
material, with their owner from the 4c ledger · Environment, in materials and surfaces ·
Light, by source, direction, quality, atmospheric particle · Style.

The Style line is READ OFF THE REFERENCE, not chosen. Identify what the reference actually
is — a phone clip, a period film look, a broadcast camera — and write the optics, motion
character and colour behaviour that match it. If it is a phone clip, write phone sensor
behaviour: mild HDR flatness, crushed shadows, slight oversharpening, auto white balance not
quite right, rolling-shutter skew, deep-ish phone depth of field. Never write "cinematic",
"dramatic" or "epic".

If the environment contains lettering, signage or brands, replace them and add the
no-readable-text rule.

```
[SHOT-BY-SHOT TIMELINE]
```
One block per shot. Header is the exact time span. Then:
- Opening line: shot size, what is in frame, foreground and background.
- Shots longer than about three seconds break into indented timecoded sub-beats.
- **Every critical beat from 4d gets its own sub-beat with timecodes to two decimals, and is
  described in more words than its duration would suggest — mechanically, in stages.** A
  0.25-second spit gets three sentences: the head lowers and the lips purse, the spit leaves
  the mouth and lands on the port with visible wetness, the head lifts. Brevity is what
  kills these beats. Do not be economical here.
- Every off-frame cause or effect from 4e stated in words.
- A separate line beginning `Camera:` — size, height, lens, movement, focus. Never folded
  into the action sentence.
- A per-shot guard where needed: "the protagonist does not appear in this shot", "hold the
  stillness, do not fill it", an identity note for a fast move.

Write "Hard cut." at the top of every shot after the first. If it is a oner, say so in the
header and state that the camera never switches position.

```
[DIALOGUE]  or  [MOUTH TIMING]
```
Per AUDIO POLICY in section 0.

GENERATE: one line per line of speech — timecode span, WHICH PARTICIPANT from [CAST] says it,
delivery note, then the line. Attribution is mandatory; an unattributed line becomes the
protagonist talking to themselves.

PLATE or HYBRID: no delivery notes. Instead, per speech span: which participant, the timecodes
the mouth opens and closes, syllable count, where the jaw drops widest, where the lips close.
For spans belonging to an unseen participant, state that the protagonist is LISTENING, not
speaking — mouth closed, eye-line toward the speaker.

Omit entirely if the clip is silent.

```
[GLOBAL SUPPLEMENT]
```
Deliberate redundancy. Everything critical appears a second time, in a different form:
- Shot count, and the cut points repeated as a bare list of numbers.
- "Do not add any action, camera move or cut that is not in the control signal. Do not insert
  extra shots to fill time." Plus, for long shots, an instruction not to cut inside them.
- **MUST NOT BE OMITTED — the critical beats from 4d, listed with their timecodes and one
  line each. State that these are short and easily lost, and that the film fails without
  them.** This line is the whole reason the section exists.
- **Causal chain in one or two sentences**, including every off-frame link.
- Presence map: which numbered shots each participant appears in, and which never appear.
- Prop persistence: each causal object, when it is in frame and when it is not.
- Identity stability restated under stress conditions with timecodes.
- Wardrobe and hairstyle consistency restated.
- Physical-weight paragraph: fabric give, hair lag behind a fast turn, weight shifts, breath,
  contact shadows.
- Any held silence or stillness restated as a directing choice.
- Sound as angle-bracket tags, one source per tag — GENERATE and HYBRID only:
  <room acoustics><prop sound><vocal event with timecode><silence span>
- State whether there is music.

```
[NEGATIVE]
```
Grouped by failure type: output format · control-signal leakage (grayscale, depth-map look,
low contrast) · text, brands and logos · identity leakage, naming the specific silhouette
features · person count and off-screen participants · **omission — name each critical beat
and each causal prop that must not vanish** · camera discipline, naming the only legal cut
points · generation artefacts (facial deformation, identity drift, morphing on the fast move,
limb distortion, extra fingers, floating props, objects disappearing between shots, flicker).
Under PLATE, add: no generated speech, music or sound.
End with the positive target: full colour, sharp, stable, contrast, aspect ratio.

## 9. Density rules

Specificity beats adjectives:

- Not "he looks surprised" → "eyebrows lift, eyes widen for two frames, lips part, then he
  swallows once"
- Not "she leans toward the connector" → "she lowers her head to the connector held in her
  right hand, purses her lips, spits once onto the metal contacts, and straightens"
- Not "the camera moves in" → "slow drift from medium to medium-close over 3 seconds"

Word budget is allocated by IMPORTANCE, not by duration. A 0.25-second beat that carries the
premise gets more words than a 3-second beat where someone stands still. If you find yourself
writing a critical beat in one clause, expand it.

Carry these six into every shot:
1. **Eye-line** — where they look, when it changes, and when it goes off-frame at whom
2. **Micro-pauses** — the half-beat before a reaction IS the reaction
3. **Weight and contact** — which foot bears weight, what touches what
4. **Breath** — chest rise, the inhale before a line, the exhale after
5. **Camera motivation** — why the camera is where it is, what starts each move
6. **Causation** — for every reaction, what caused it, stated even when invisible

Leave out: anything you did not observe, the reference performer's identity, music unless the
reference has it, subtitles.

## 12. Failure modes

- **Describing without understanding.** An accurate account of the motion that misses what
  the clip is about. Section 4 exists for this and is not optional.
- **The vanishing micro-action.** A spit, a wink, a tap — too short and too small to survive
  summarising, and the entire point of the clip. Give it more words, not fewer.
- **The erased participant.** An off-screen voice or hand belongs to a person. Miss them and
  a conversation becomes a monologue.
- **The orphan prop.** An object with no owner and no exit disappears between shots.
- **The unmotivated reaction.** A reaction whose cause is off-frame or hidden in a cut, with
  nothing written to connect them. The model will invent a cause or drop the reaction.
- **One viewing.** Watching once and writing a plausible summary.
- **Whip mistaken for a cut.** A fast turn is not an edit. Check the background.
- **Smoothing.** "He moves across the room" instead of the weight shifts and pauses.
- **Silent invention.** Any beat, prop or word you did not observe must be marked.
- **Adjective drift.** Replace every adjective with an observable.
- **Empty deny lists.** The most common cause of a grey cast or a studio background.
- **Ignoring the silhouette.** The depth map still holds the reference performer's outline.
- **Filling the pauses.** A second of stillness is a directing choice.
